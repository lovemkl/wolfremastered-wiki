Fabletown runs on its people: a community of storybook refugees packed into 1980s Manhattan tenements, every one of them carrying the weight of a famous past and the rent of a hard present. The Wolf Among Us Remastered gives the full cast the definitive presentation — rebuilt visuals doing justice to faces the originals rendered in 2013's polygons — and this guide profiles every major player in Bigby's season. Spoiler level: roles and relationships, no case turns.

## Snow White

Deputy to the mayor and the town's actual spine. Snow White in Fabletown is not the fairy tale's waiting princess but the woman who keeps a hidden civilization's paperwork, budget and truce from collapsing — competent, principled and carrying her own famous history with a steadiness the town leans on more than it knows. Her relationship with Bigby is the season's central partnership: she is the person most convinced the sheriff is real and the wolf is a choice, which makes her faith the story's quiet stake in Bigby's better nature. Across the five episodes, Snow is also the player's most consistent window into what Fabletown is supposed to be — the ideal the community keeps failing and keeps trying.

## Ichabod Crane

The deputy mayor himself — technically Snow's superior, functionally her obstacle. Crane is the Legend of Sleepy Hollow's schoolteacher grown into a bureaucrat: vain, cowardly, self-serving and endlessly, cringingly concerned with appearances. The game uses him as the institutional face of everything wrong with Fabletown's government — procedure without courage, authority without accountability — and the season's interrogation scenes make him one of its most reliable sources of dark comedy and genuine complication. His handling of the murder case is a masterclass in institutional failure, and Bigby's patience with him is tested on a schedule.

## Beauty and the Beast

The fairy tale's famous couple, married and struggling with it. In Fabletown, Beauty and Beast are a young couple money-stressed and secret-keeping — Beast's jealousy reawakens his transformation, and Beauty's attempts to manage their finances without telling him run straight through the season's black market. Their arc is the game's most grounded thread about what the truce demands of people: two immortally famous lovers discovering that exile did not exempt them from ordinary marriage problems, just moved them to a Manhattan apartment. Bigby keeps crossing their path because everyone in financial desperation eventually crosses the same paths.

## Mr. Toad and Toad Jr.

The landlord of a rundown tenement building and the season's clearest emblem of the glamour economy's cruelty. Toad of Toad Hall — the reckless aristocrat of The Wind in the Willows, now a slum landlord — cannot pass as human and cannot afford the glamour that would let his son TJ live a normal childhood. Their situation is the game's poverty thesis in miniature: the Laws require what the market prices beyond reach, and the community's most vulnerable pay the difference. Bigby's recurring friction with Toad — the sheriff's warnings about TJ's visibility versus a father's pride and poverty — is one of the season's most quietly effective choice canvases.

## Colin

One of the three little pigs — specifically, the pig whose house of straw Bigby blew down. Colin crashes on Bigby's couch, drinks his beer, and serves as the season's Greek chorus: the victim-turned-friend who needles the wolf about his reputation, his loneliness and his choices with the entitlement only an old adversary turned houseguest can carry. The conversations with Colin at the end of long days are the game's decompression scenes, and the writing uses them to let Bigby — and the player — sit with what the day required before the next day arrives.

## Holly and the Trip Trap Bar

Holly, the troll who runs the Trip Trap, anchors the season's working-class Fabletown — the bar where the community's margins drink, where information costs a round, and where grief becomes political as the case's victims land closer to her family. Her sister's involvement in the glamour trade's sad economy gives the middle episodes their emotional weight, and Holly's scenes carry the game's clearest argument that the town's protection has always been selective. Around her table sit the recurring margins — Grendel, the ancient monster now nursing grudges and beers in equal measure, and the community of unglamoured fables the Laws pretend do not exist.

## The Underworld

Opposite the tenements sits the season's other Manhattan: the Pudding & Pie and its manager Georgie Porgie, the businesses that profit from fables with nothing left to sell but their famous faces, and — behind all of it — the Crooked Man, the figure whose name the season spends three episodes earning. The antagonist's design belongs with the case reveals, but his function belongs here: the Crooked Man is what Fabletown's economy produces when desperation is monetized — order, of a kind, purchased at the price the whole season exists to name. Nerissa, the mysterious dancer whose counsel reaches Bigby at the moments it matters most, completes the underworld's cast and carries the season's final ambiguity on her shoulders.

## The Cast as a System in The Wolf Among Us Remastered

Worth saying plainly: the cast of The Wolf Among Us Remastered works as a system, not a gallery. The season's design routes Bigby through every stratum of Fabletown on purpose — the government (Crane, Snow), the working tenements (Toad, the Woodlands' residents), the bar economy (Holly, Grendel) and the underworld (Georgie, the Crooked Man) — so that by the finale the player has personally witnessed what each layer hides. The murder case is the vehicle; the community portrait is the cargo. That structure is why the cast guide matters more here than in most adventure games: understanding The Wolf Among Us Remastered's story means understanding who everyone is to everyone else, and the game deliberately makes you assemble that map yourself, one interrogation at a time.

The remaster's complete-season packaging strengthens the system reading. In the original's episodic rollout, months between episodes softened the web of relationships; played start to finish in the remaster's integrated format — with the rebuilt visuals keeping faces consistent and legible — the connections land as one continuous social fabric. New players get the cast as the writers actually built it; returning players get to check the seams they remembered against the ones that were really there. Either way, walk Fabletown slowly: in The Wolf Among Us Remastered, every face in the crowd is somebody's story, and the season tells most of them if you keep stopping to talk.

## FAQ

**Are all the characters from real fairy tales?** Yes — the game's cast is drawn from the Fables comics' policy of public-domain storybook figures, with their famous histories intact and their presents ruthlessly reinvented. Half the game's pleasure is the recognition: who is this, and what has exile done to them?

**Which characters return from the Fables comics?** Most of the principal cast — Bigby, Snow, Crane, Beauty and Beast, Toad — originate in Bill Willingham's comics, with the game's versions tuned for its noir. Readers get the deeper cuts; newcomers lose nothing.

**Who is the most important character besides Bigby?** Snow White — as partner, conscience and the season's other protagonist in all but playability. The story is as much about what she believes the town can be as what Bigby fears he is.

**Does the cast change between the original and the remaster?** No — The Wolf Among Us Remastered presents the original season's complete cast unchanged, with rebuilt visuals giving the familiar faces their best-ever rendering.

**Where do the relationships pay off?** In Cry Wolf — the finale reads the season's accumulated relationships back to you, and who stands where in the last chapter depends on how you treated all of them. The cast guide's whole point: in this game, everyone was keeping score.
