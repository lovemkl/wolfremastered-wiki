The Wolf Among Us Remastered is a game about decisions: hundreds of them across five episodes, most small, some enormous, all of them assembling the version of Bigby Wolf — and the version of Fabletown — you carry into the finale. This guide explains how the choice system actually works, which decisions the season is genuinely built around, and how your accumulated file reads back at the ending. Full spoiler warning: this page discusses decisions and consequences openly — first-time players should use it between chapters, not before them.

## How the System Works

Three mechanics carry everything. **Timed dialogue**: most conversational choices run under a countdown, and letting it expire is itself an answer — Bigby saying nothing is always a thing the room notices. **The violence dial**: across the season, Bigby is repeatedly offered escalation — beat this man further or stop, destroy this room or leave it, tear or restrain — and the game tracks the pattern rather than each incident. **Reputation, not branches**: the season does not fork into different stories; it colors one story with your decisions — who trusts the sheriff, who fears him, who is alive to appear in later scenes. The right frame: you are choosing who Bigby is, and the story is choosing what happens to whoever that turns out to be.

## The Categories That Matter

The decisions that shape the season sort into four buckets, and knowing them changes how a first playthrough feels:

**Mercy versus force.** The season's most frequent choice: given someone at your mercy — a suspect, a witness, a punch-drunk antagonist — how much wolf comes out? The game never marks these on a morality dial; it shows the room's reaction and remembers your pattern. Communities in The Wolf Among Us Remastered treat a frightening sheriff and a restrained one as genuinely different facts of life, and later scenes price your earlier choices accordingly.

**Who you protect.** Several key decisions concern disclosure and loyalty: what you report, to whom, and whose secrets you spend to advance the case. The town is small and everyone talks; protecting one person routinely costs standing with another. The season's best quiet dilemma — a medical secret a vulnerable character asks Bigby to keep, against the investigation's need for it — is exactly this bucket.

**The relationships you invest in.** Time given to Snow, patience spent on Toad, honesty offered to Holly — the supporting cast's arc positions in the finale absorb how you treated them all season. None of it is transactional bookkeeping; it is the accumulated sense the town has made of you.

**The finale's direct questions.** Cry Wolf hands the season's biggest decisions to you explicitly — what to do with the antagonist at the moment of judgment, and what Bigby says the wolf is for. The ending variations draw on the whole file: your temperament history, your relationships and your final calls, assembled into one of several distinct conclusions.

## What Carries Between Episodes

Everything. The season maintains one continuous file — choices, relationships, who lived and who did not — and each episode opens on the Fabletown your previous chapters made. Practically: early-game decisions have late-game texture (people you were kind to in Faith surface in Cry Wolf as people willing to stand up for the sheriff), and the finale reads the longest arcs. This is why the replay is a different experience rather than a do-over: a second run's Bigby starts with your knowledge of which moments were the load-bearing ones, and the game shows you exactly how many of them there were.

## First-Run Advice

Three rules for a first playthrough. **Decide as Bigby, under the clock** — the timed system is the game's soul, and optimizing it away is optimizing away the experience. **Do not chase a "best" outcome** — the season is deliberately written without a clean-hands route; the choices cost, the writing knows it, and the point is which costs you accept. **Save this guide for between chapters** — the game's first run is the only one you get to live through unknowing, and the not-knowing is load-bearing.

For replays: chapter-select the pivot episodes rather than restarting the season, play the opposite temperament deliberately (a restrained run after a violent one, or the reverse), and use the other presentation mode — original color or Noir Mode — as the second run's lens. The game is built for exactly this: two runs, two wolves, one story that holds both.

## FAQ

**Can I get a "perfect" ending?** No — and the absence is deliberate. The finale's variations are all defensible and all costly; the season asks which Bigby you stand behind, not which answer is correct.

**How many endings are there?** Several distinct finale variations, driven by the judgment decisions plus your accumulated relationships and temperament. Two players with different seasons can reach the same final call and still see different final chapters around it.

**Do dialogue choices really matter, or just the big ones?** The big ones move plot; the dialogue ones move Bigby. Both are real — the game's memory is longer than players expect, and rooms react to the sheriff your small choices built.

**What happens if I let timers expire?** Silence is a choice — sometimes wise, sometimes expensive, always noticed. The game accounts for quiet Bigbys as completely as talkative ones.

**Is one temperament "canon"?** The season's marketing leans restrained-but-capable, and the sequel's developers will presumably choose a baseline — but the game itself never endorses a version. Your Bigby is the canon of your file.

Every choice was the game asking one question in twenty costumes. The ending is your answer.
